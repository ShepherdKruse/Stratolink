The dashboard draws one continuous line for every flight. The balloon doesn’t. It only tells us where it is when a gateway on the ground hears a packet, and over an ocean nobody is listening. Stratolink 3 flew for twelve days in May and was silent for eight of them. This is how the rest of the line gets drawn, and what I changed about it this month.

What the balloon tells us

Stratolink 3 launched from San Francisco on May 17 and sent its last packet with a position on May 29, from just outside Málaga. In between we received 255 packets carrying GPS coordinates, but only 39 distinct positions. The GNSS module went long stretches without a fresh fix and the firmware kept sending the last one it had, a bug Teddy describes in his dev log. For drawing a path, a repeated fix is a packet with no position in it.

The timing is what shapes the map. The flight lasted 291 hours, and 267 of them fall inside four gaps longer than six hours. One gap holds 201 of those hours: the last fix before it came from New Mexico at 22:28 UTC on May 19, and the first fix after it came from Spain at 07:38 UTC on May 28. The two points are 8,271 km apart, which works out to 11.4 m/s over the gap. The winds at the balloon’s altitude were blowing at 20 to 30 m/s for most of that week. So it did not go straight, and nothing from the balloon says where it went instead.

We do know how high it was. At float the pressure sensor put it near 280 hPa, between about 9.5 and 10 km, a little lower at night. The weather models publish winds at pressure levels, and a balloon floating at a known pressure is a passive tracer of the wind there. That is the basis of everything below.

The reconstruction sorts the gaps by length. Under 20 minutes is a straight line; at 20 m/s that is at most 24 km. Between 20 minutes and six hours is a particle bridge, described next. Six hours and up is the long-gap method that takes up most of this post. On Stratolink 3 that comes to 246 short gaps, 4 medium ones, and the 4 long ones that hold 92% of the flight.

Winds, cut into a tube

The winds come from NOAA’s Global Forecast System, GFS. It runs four times a day on a 0.25° grid, about 28 km at these latitudes, and publishes wind components on standard pressure levels, hourly out to five days. We read the east and north wind at the levels around the balloon’s float pressure: for the forecast, the two bracketing levels, 300 and 250 hPa for this flight, blended to 281 hPa; for the reconstruction, those two plus 200 and 350 hPa, kept separate so each particle can ride its own level. For times in the past we use the model’s own analyses, every three hours; for the future, the latest cycle’s hourly forecast.

NOAA puts every run in a public S3 bucket with an index of the byte range of each field, so we can fetch just the two wind fields at just the two levels we need, for free and without an API key.1 Each field is still the whole globe, a million grid points, so the per-balloon cube is cut out of it: a stack of time slices, each a small grid of U and V stored as 16-bit integers.

Until this month that cube was one box around the whole mission plus padding. For a flight from California to Spain the box spans an ocean and most of a continent, and to keep it under the point budget the grid had to be coarsened to 0.5° or 1°, throwing away most of the model’s resolution exactly where the balloon spent the most time. The forward forecast could also drift out of the box, and a wind sample outside the box quietly returns the value at the nearest edge. The balloon doesn’t fly on an edge-clamped wind.

The cube is now a tube. Each three-hour slice is its own box at 0.25°, padded to hold the reconstruction’s particle cloud for the gap it sits in: ±9° around gaps up to 60 hours, growing to ±16° through the 201-hour one. Around short gaps the box sits on the straight line between the fixes. Inside a long gap it sits on the path the wind itself takes: forward from the fix before the gap and backward from the fix after it, both through the middle. My first version centered every box on the straight line, and that turned out to matter more than anything else in this post; more on that below. There are two cubes per balloon: a three-hourly one along the whole mission for the reconstruction, about 22 MB for Stratolink 3 with its four levels, and an hourly one from the last fix forward for the forecast, 1.8 MB.

Short gaps

For a gap between 20 minutes and six hours the balloon can’t have gone far, and the bridge is simple. Solve first for the small speed and heading correction that makes a path driven by the wind land exactly on the next fix. Then launch 200 particles from each fix, each with its own random speed factor, heading offset and altitude; run the forward ones toward the far fix and the backward ones through time-reversed winds toward the near one; weight each by how close it lands to its target, with a 35 km tolerance; and average. The result is a gently curved line that follows the wind and starts and ends on the measured positions.

The eight-day gap

That recipe fails over eight days. Launch particles from New Mexico and hope that some land within a few tens of kilometers of a point in Spain 200 hours later, and none do. The ones that would have needed to draw exactly the right sequence of perturbations for a week, and there aren’t enough particles in the world for that.

The method we had been running tried to get around this with a pull. Each of 600 particles was integrated forward through the winds with a small nudge toward the far fix at every step, and its weight was penalized for every bit of nudging it needed. The trouble is that 600 steps of penalties add up. The log-weights of the particles ended up spanning tens of units, which means one particle holds essentially all of the weight. The line we drew was that one particle’s path, and when the job ran again a different particle won.

I assumed the fix was more particles. It isn’t. The effective sample size, the number of particles that actually contribute to the average, was 1 at 600 particles and still 2 at 60,000. Pick a particle count in the figure above and add reruns: the line moves by hundreds of kilometers between them at every count, and in one pair at 6,000 particles by more than 1,700. More particles make a more expensive lottery.

Meeting in the middle

The replacement drops the pull entirely. A forward filter runs 2,000 particles from the New Mexico fix toward Spain, and a backward filter runs 2,000 from Spain toward New Mexico through time-reversed winds. Neither knows about the other end. Each particle follows the wind with its own small errors in speed, heading and pressure level, which drift and recover over a day or two. The only weighting inside a filter is a penalty for reading wind from outside the tube, and whenever a filter’s effective sample size drops below half its population it is resampled, so a cloud never collapses onto a few lineages.

The two clouds meet at the midpoint of the gap, around hour 100, and overlap for eight hours either side of it. Every forward particle is paired with every backward particle, and each pair is weighted by how closely its two halves track each other across the overlap. Two halves that cross at the midpoint heading in different directions pull apart over those eight hours, so the match couples direction as well as position. Inside the window the halves are blended so the joined path is continuous.

Those pair weights are the posterior. 2,000 forward particles times 2,000 backward ones is four million candidate bridges for the cost of integrating two clouds, and on this gap about 35,000 of them carry meaningful weight. Switch the earlier figure to the two filters and add reruns: the stored runs agree to within about 100 km along the gap, where the forward sampler’s disagreed by a thousand, and the smoother runs in a few seconds.

Following the wind

The two filters fixed the lottery, but the line they drew was stable in the wrong way. I measured the angle between every step of every posterior bridge and the wind at that place and time. On the eight-day gap, one step in ten was more than 80° off the wind, and about 6% of steps ran against it. A balloon can’t do that. It goes where the air goes.

Two things were letting the particles cross the flow. The first was the perturbation. Each particle’s heading took a random step every four hours, and the steps added up without limit; by the middle of the gap a typical particle was flying 118° off the wind. The second was the tube. Every box sat on the straight line from New Mexico to Spain, moving at the 11.4 m/s that line implies. The GFS winds at 281 hPa do something else entirely: they carry the balloon to the middle of the Atlantic in two days, hold it for four in a slow loop west of the Azores, and then push it into Spain. That path left the boxes within its first day and didn’t come back until its sixth. Particles that followed the wind left the tube and were weighted out, and the ones that survived were the ones whose random headings kept them inside it.

So now each particle’s errors are bounded. The heading offset drifts but always relaxes back toward the wind, with a spread of 12°, the same as the forecast uses, and the speed factor does the same with a 15% spread. The spreads come from the stretches of the flight where we had clean fixes a few hours apart: there the balloon moved within a few degrees of the GFS wind and at 0.8 to 1.1 times its speed. Each particle also rides its own pressure level, starting at the pressure the balloon reported at its fix and wandering by a few hPa over a couple of days, which is how much Stratolink 3’s pressure changed across the gap. And inside a long gap the tube follows the wind’s path from both ends, as described above.

The result is in the third tab of the figure above and the figures below. Nine in ten steps of the posterior are now within 20° of the wind, about what the forecast ensemble shows, and the bridges move at the wind’s speed. The forward and backward clouds meet within about 15 km of each other, and the line moves by about 50 km between reruns. It goes up the jet over Newfoundland, loops near the Azores and comes into Spain from the southwest. Run the GFS winds forward from New Mexico or backward from Spain with no perturbation at all and you get the same route. The eight-day gap, which I had come to think of as unknowable, is explained by the winds at the balloon’s own altitude.

What a particle assumes

Each particle moves with the GFS wind at its own pressure, interpolated between the four surrounding grid points, the two surrounding time slices and the two surrounding pressure levels, in 20-minute steps. Its heading offset and speed factor are mean-reverting random processes with spreads of 12° and 15% and a memory of about 18 hours. Its pressure starts at the reading from its fix and wanders with an 8 hPa spread and a memory of about two days, around a line between the readings at the two ends of the gap.

None of this models the balloon beyond its altitude. A balloon at 280 hPa is treated as a passive tracer of the wind there, and the perturbations stand in for everything the model doesn’t resolve, from the wind between grid points to the errors in the model itself. The backward filter is the same code with the sign of time flipped: a tracer that moves with the wind can be run backward exactly. If the winds can’t connect the two ends of a gap at all, the smoother now says so instead of drawing a line.

Which line to draw

Having a posterior is nice, but the dashboard draws one line. The obvious choice is the posterior mean, the weighted average position of every bridge at every step. It was the first thing I drew, and the more I looked at it the less I liked it.

The problem is that the average of several real routes is not a route. With the wandering headings and the straight-line tube, the bridges sorted themselves into groups by the fourth day of the gap: three clusters at hour 120, 78% southeast of Newfoundland and the rest well to either side, with the mean in the empty water between them. At those hours between 0% and 16% of the draws were within 300 km of the mean. It was a smooth line through places the balloon probably wasn’t.

Those clusters turned out to be a symptom of the particles crossing the wind. With the current smoother the draws stay in one bundle at every hour, shown above, and the mean, the medoid and the drawn line all sit inside it, typically 50 to 70 km apart. But I didn’t know that when I chose the line, and other gaps on other flights may genuinely split, so the choice still matters.

The alternatives I tried each failed differently. Picking the single most central bridge, the medoid, gives a real route inside the biggest cluster, but it is a discrete choice between near-tied candidates and flips between them: by 100 to 170 km just from redrawing the samples, and by up to 1,000 km on a rerun. Grouping the bridges into route families and taking the biggest family sounds right, but the families weren’t stable either; the biggest one’s share moved between 34% and 76% across runs of the same gap.

What we settled on sits between the two. Find the medoid, take the 10% of bridges that track it most closely over the whole gap, 200 of the 2,000, and average those step by step. When the posterior splits, the line stays inside the dominant mode; when it doesn’t, it lands next to the mean.

The dashboard still shows one line, and I want to be clear about what it is: the center of the bundle of routes the winds allow, not a measurement. In the middle of this gap the posterior is about 700 km wide, down from 1,900 km before the particles followed the wind. The line is our best single guess through that corridor, and showing the corridor is the thing I most want to change next.

Where it goes next

The forward forecast is the same integration pointed at the future, with one change. Instead of perturbing a single wind field, each path rides a different model run. NOAA’s Global Ensemble Forecast System, GEFS, publishes 31 members, each started from slightly different initial conditions, and its machine-learning counterpart AIGEFS publishes another 31.2 We cut a tube for every member and integrate the balloon from the last fix through each one, which gives 62 paths that disagree exactly as much as the weather models do.

The line the dashboard draws is the per-hour median of the members, and the forecast ends at the first hour where the 75th-percentile distance of the members from that median passes 2,000 km. Past that point the members are spread across a continent and the median isn’t predicting anything. For Stratolink 3’s last fix near Málaga on May 29, the members ran east across the Mediterranean, Asia and the Pacific, and their spread crossed 2,000 km on June 9, ten and a half days out, over Utah.

Because Stratolink 3 never sent another position, the stretch from that last fix to now is itself a forecast, and the dashboard draws it as one. The route at the top of Teddy’s June post came from an earlier experiment with this machinery, pushed on for weeks toward a guessed endpoint. The forecast now stops when the models stop agreeing, which is the honest place to stop.

The plumbing

All of this runs on a GitHub Actions runner, four times a day. A run starts by deciding which balloons need work. A balloon whose last fix is more than 15 days old and whose stored forecast already ends at its divergence point cannot change, so it is skipped, and when the fleet is quiet the run plans nothing, downloads nothing and exits. When a balloon is flying, the runner pulls the wind fields it needs from NOAA’s bucket by byte range, builds the two cubes plus a tube per ensemble member, runs the reconstruction and the forecast, and writes one JSON file per balloon, about half a megabyte, to Vercel Blob. The dashboard reads that file and draws.

Each bridge is cached by a hash of its two fixes, the pressure level and the method version, so a rerun only recomputes the gaps that changed. For Stratolink 3 the whole compute, reconstruction and forecast together, takes about fifteen seconds on the runner. The ingest takes longer, and almost all of it is waiting on S3 for the member cubes; when a few members don’t arrive inside the step’s time budget, the forecast runs on the ones that did.

Where the code is

Everything is in the Stratolink repository, under web/. The ingest is scripts/gfs_ingest.py, with gefs_ingest.py and aigefs_ingest.py for the ensemble members. The cube format and wind sampling are in lib/wind/windCube.ts, the short and medium gaps in lib/wind/pathReconstruction.ts, the two-filter smoother and the trimmed local mean in lib/wind/pathReconstructionLongGap.ts, and the forecast in lib/wind/monteCarloForecast.ts. The schedule is .github/workflows/gfs-ingest.yml.

What’s next

Three things, in the order I think they matter.

Show the corridor. We have 2,000 plausible routes for every long gap and draw one. I’ve prototyped ribbons, sample bundles and daily uncertainty ellipses on the dashboard map, and none of them is on the site yet, mostly because a band 700 km wide across the Atlantic has to be drawn carefully to read as “somewhere in here” rather than as noise.

Calibration. Each particle now rides its own pressure level and follows the wind within spreads I set from this one flight, which had only a handful of clean fix pairs to learn from. Replaying more flights, and the stretches of them where we did get positions, should pin down how far a balloon really strays from the model wind, and whether the level that best explains its motion differs from the one its sensor reports.

More fixes. Everything here gets easier when the balloon talks more often, and Teddy’s v1 firmware rejects stale fixes so that a packet with a position means a position. The Baja leg of this flight, where we had a position every few minutes, needed none of this.

The positions we did receive, and the line through them, are on Stratolink 3’s flight. Shepherd’s Baja Run covers the first two days, and Teddy’s Looking for Stratolink 3 picks up after the last packet.
