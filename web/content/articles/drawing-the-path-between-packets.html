The dashboard draws one continuous line for every flight. The balloon doesn’t. It only tells us where it is when a gateway on the ground hears a packet, and over an ocean nobody is listening. Stratolink 3 flew for twelve days in May and was silent for eight of them. This is how the rest of the line gets drawn: not as a forecast, but as the most probable path between two points we know.

What the balloon tells us

Stratolink 3 launched from San Francisco on May 17 and sent its last packet with a position on May 29, from just outside Málaga. In between we received 255 packets carrying GPS coordinates, but only 39 distinct positions. The GNSS module went long stretches without a fresh fix and the firmware kept sending the last one it had, a bug Teddy describes in his dev log. For drawing a path, a repeated fix is a packet with no position in it.

The timing is what shapes the map. The flight lasted 291 hours, and 267 of them fall inside four gaps longer than six hours. One gap holds 201 of those hours: the last fix before it came from New Mexico at 22:28 UTC on May 19, and the first fix after it came from Spain at 07:38 UTC on May 28. The two points are 8,271 km apart, which works out to 11.4 m/s, while the winds at the balloon’s altitude were blowing at 20 to 30 m/s for most of that week. So it did not go straight, and nothing from the balloon says where it went instead. What we do have is both ends: where it went quiet, and where it turned up again eight days later.

What we do know is how high it was. At float the pressure sensor put it near 280 hPa, between about 9.5 and 10 km. The weather models publish winds at pressure levels, and a balloon floating at a known pressure is a passive tracer of the wind there: it goes where the air goes. Everything below follows from that.

The reconstruction sorts the gaps by length. Under 20 minutes is a straight line; at 20 m/s that is at most 24 km. Between 20 minutes and six hours is a short particle bridge. Six hours and up is the long-gap method that takes up most of this post. On Stratolink 3 that comes to 246 short gaps, 4 medium ones, and the 4 long ones that hold 92% of the flight.

Winds, cut into a tube

The winds come from NOAA’s Global Forecast System, GFS, which runs four times a day on a 0.25° grid, about 28 km at these latitudes. NOAA puts every run in a public S3 bucket with an index of the byte range of each field, so we can fetch just the wind fields we need, for free and without an API key.1 For the reconstruction we read four pressure levels around the balloon’s float, 200, 250, 300 and 350 hPa, from the model’s analyses every three hours; for the forecast, the latest cycle’s hourly winds blended to the float pressure.

Each field is the whole globe, a million grid points, so the winds a balloon needs are cut into a tube: a stack of three-hour slices, each a box at full 0.25° resolution. Around a short gap the box sits on the line between the two fixes. Inside a long gap it follows the winds: before the box is cut, a small pilot cloud of particles is run forward from the fix before the gap and backward from the fix after it, and each box is sized to hold those clouds over the six hours it is read. That matters. The winds took Stratolink 3 to the middle of the Atlantic in two days and then held it for four in a slow loop west of the Azores; a tube laid along the straight line to Spain would have been somewhere else entirely by the second day. Around every box the cube also keeps the wind at a coarser 1.5° over most of the hemisphere, so a particle that wanders out of the box still rides real winds. For Stratolink 3 the reconstruction cube is about 22 MB and the hourly forecast cube 1.8 MB.

Short gaps

For a gap between 20 minutes and six hours the balloon can’t have gone far, and the bridge is simple. Solve for the small speed and heading correction that makes a path driven by the wind land on the next fix. Then launch 200 particles from each fix with small random variations, run the forward ones toward the far fix and the backward ones through time-reversed winds toward the near one, weight each by how close it lands, and average. The result is a gently curved line that follows the wind and starts and ends on the measured positions.

Long gaps: meeting in the middle

A long gap is not a forecasting problem. A forecast knows only where the balloon is now, so it has to look forward, and small differences in the wind grow until the possibilities span an ocean. Here we know both ends: where the balloon was when it went quiet, and where it was when it spoke again 201 hours later. The question isn’t where the balloon could have gone. It’s which path between those two points the winds make most probable.

The difference is easy to see. Run the particles forward from New Mexico with no knowledge of Spain, and after 201 hours they are spread across the Atlantic, Europe and North Africa: a median 3,590 km from where the balloon actually was, with fewer than one in two hundred within 300 km of it. The fix in Spain is the most valuable thing we know about the gap, and a forward run throws it away. So instead of aiming from one end, we start from both.

A forward filter runs 8,000 particles from the New Mexico fix toward Spain, and a backward filter runs 8,000 from Spain toward New Mexico through time-reversed winds (shorter gaps settle with 2,000). Neither knows about the other end. Each particle follows the wind with small errors of its own in heading, speed and pressure level, errors that drift and then recover over a day or two, standing in for everything the model doesn’t capture. A particle that strays out of the box keeps going on the coarser winds; only one that leaves the stored winds altogether is weighted down. When too few particles in a filter carry most of the weight, the filter is resampled so the cloud never collapses onto a handful of them.

The two clouds meet at the middle of the gap, around hour 100, and overlap for eight hours either side of it. Every forward particle is paired with every backward particle near it, and each pair is weighted by how closely its two halves track each other across the overlap. Two halves that cross at the midpoint heading in different directions pull apart over those eight hours, so the match rewards agreeing on direction as well as position. Inside the window the two halves are blended into one continuous path.

Those pair weights are the answer. 8,000 forward particles times 8,000 backward ones is 64 million candidate bridges for the cost of integrating two clouds, and on this gap about 600,000 of them carry meaningful weight. Together they are the set of routes the winds allow between the two fixes, each weighted by how plausible it is; the dashboard draws from 2,000 of them. The whole reconstruction runs in well under a minute.

What a particle assumes

Each particle moves with the GFS wind at its own pressure, interpolated between the surrounding grid points, time slices and pressure levels, in 20-minute steps. Its heading offset and speed factor wander around zero with spreads of 12° and 15% and a memory of about 18 hours. Its pressure starts at the reading from its fix and wanders with an 8 hPa spread and a memory of about two days, around a line between the readings at the two ends of the gap.

Those spreads come from the flight itself. Where Stratolink 3 sent clean fixes a few hours apart, it moved within a few degrees of the GFS wind direction and at 0.8 to 1.1 times its speed, and its pressure drifted from about 288 to 281 hPa across the eight-day gap. The backward filter is the same code with the sign of time flipped: a tracer that moves with the wind can be run backward exactly.

Checking it against the wind

The first thing I check on any reconstruction is whether the balloon behaves like a balloon. For every step of every bridge, measure the angle between the direction it moved and the wind at that place and time. On the eight-day gap, nine steps in ten are within 20° of the wind, about the same as the forecast ensemble shows, and the bridges move at the wind’s speed. If the particles could fly across the flow, this is where it would show.

The other checks are about stability. The forward and backward clouds meet within about 45 km of each other, the drawn line moves by about 10 km when the whole thing is rerun, and if the winds can’t connect the two ends of a gap at all, the reconstruction says so instead of drawing a line. The simplest check is the most convincing one: run the GFS winds forward from New Mexico, or backward from Spain, with no randomness at all, and you get the same route. Up the jet over Newfoundland, a loop near the Azores, and into Spain from the southwest. The eight-day gap is explained by the winds at the balloon’s own altitude.

One line from 2,000

The dashboard draws one line, so the bridges have to be boiled down to one. Because they form a single bundle, the line is simply their average position at each hour.

That isn’t always safe. If the bridges split, say around a patch of slow air, the average of two routes runs between them, through places the balloon never was. So the reconstruction checks: every two hours it compares how many bridges lie within 300 km of the average with how many lie within 300 km of the center of the biggest bundle. If the average ever holds fewer than half as many, the routes have split, and the line follows the biggest bundle instead. On Stratolink 3 no gap splits, and the line moves by under 20 km when the whole job is rerun.

I want to be clear about what that line is: the center of the routes the winds allow, not a measurement. In the middle of the gap, 90% of the bridges fall within 500 to 800 km of it. The line is our best single guess through a corridor that wide, and showing the corridor is the thing I most want to change next.

Where it goes next

The forward forecast is the same integration pointed at the future, with one change. Instead of perturbing a single wind field, each path rides a different model run. NOAA’s Global Ensemble Forecast System, GEFS, publishes 31 members, each started from slightly different initial conditions, and its machine-learning counterpart AIGEFS publishes another 31.2 We cut a tube for every member and integrate the balloon from the last fix through each one, which gives 62 paths that disagree exactly as much as the weather models do.

The line the dashboard draws is the per-hour median of the members, and the forecast ends at the first hour where the 75th-percentile distance of the members from that median passes 2,000 km. Past that point the members are spread across a continent and the median isn’t predicting anything. For Stratolink 3’s last fix near Málaga on May 29, the members ran east across the Mediterranean, Asia and the Pacific, and their spread crossed 2,000 km on June 9, ten and a half days out, over Utah.

Because Stratolink 3 never sent another position, the stretch from that last fix to now is itself a forecast, and the dashboard draws it as one. The route at the top of Teddy’s June post came from an earlier experiment with this machinery, pushed on for weeks toward a guessed endpoint. The forecast now stops when the models stop agreeing, which is the honest place to stop.

The plumbing

All of this runs on a GitHub Actions runner, four times a day. A run starts by deciding which balloons need work. A balloon whose last fix is more than 15 days old and whose stored forecast already ends at its divergence point cannot change, so it is skipped, and when the fleet is quiet the run plans nothing, downloads nothing and exits. When a balloon is flying, the runner pulls the wind fields it needs from NOAA’s bucket by byte range, builds the two cubes plus a tube per ensemble member, runs the reconstruction and the forecast, and writes one JSON file per balloon, about half a megabyte, to Vercel Blob. The dashboard reads that file and draws.

Each bridge is cached by a hash of its two fixes, the pressure level and the method version, so a rerun only recomputes the gaps that changed. For Stratolink 3 the whole compute, reconstruction and forecast together, takes under a minute on the runner. The ingest takes longer, and almost all of it is waiting on S3 for the member cubes; when a few members don’t arrive inside the step’s time budget, the forecast runs on the ones that did.

Where the code is

Everything is in the Stratolink repository, under web/. The ingest is scripts/gfs_ingest.py, with gefs_ingest.py and aigefs_ingest.py for the ensemble members. The cube format and wind sampling are in lib/wind/windCube.ts, the short and medium gaps in lib/wind/pathReconstruction.ts, the long-gap filters and the drawn line in lib/wind/pathReconstructionLongGap.ts, and the forecast in lib/wind/monteCarloForecast.ts. The schedule is .github/workflows/gfs-ingest.yml.

What’s next

Three things, in the order I think they matter.

Show the corridor. We have 2,000 plausible routes for every long gap and draw one. I’ve prototyped ribbons, sample bundles and daily uncertainty ellipses on the dashboard map, and none of them is on the site yet, mostly because a band several hundred kilometers wide across the Atlantic has to be drawn carefully to read as “somewhere in here” rather than as noise.

Calibration. The spreads each particle carries come from one flight, which had only a handful of clean fix pairs to learn from. Replaying more flights, and the stretches of them where we did get positions, should pin down how far a balloon really strays from the model wind.

More fixes. Everything here gets easier when the balloon talks more often, and Teddy’s v1 firmware rejects stale fixes so that a packet with a position means a position. The Baja leg of this flight, where we had a position every few minutes, needed none of this.

The positions we did receive, and the line through them, are on Stratolink 3’s flight. Shepherd’s Baja Run covers the first two days, and Teddy’s Looking for Stratolink 3 picks up after the last packet.
