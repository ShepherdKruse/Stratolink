The dashboard draws one continuous line for every flight. The balloon doesn’t. It only tells us where it is when a gateway on the ground hears a packet, and over an ocean nobody is listening. Stratolink 3 flew for twelve days in May and was silent for eight of them. This is how the rest of the line gets drawn, and what I changed about it this month.

What the balloon tells us

Stratolink 3 launched from San Francisco on May 17 and sent its last packet with a position on May 29, from just outside Málaga. In between we received 255 packets carrying GPS coordinates, but only 39 distinct positions. The GNSS module went long stretches without a fresh fix and the firmware kept sending the last one it had, a bug Teddy describes in his dev log. For drawing a path, a repeated fix is a packet with no position in it.

The timing is what shapes the map. The flight lasted 291 hours, and 267 of them fall inside four gaps longer than six hours. One gap holds 201 of those hours: the last fix before it came from New Mexico at 22:28 UTC on May 19, and the first fix after it came from Spain at 07:38 UTC on May 28. The two points are 8,271 km apart, which works out to 11.4 m/s over the gap. The winds at the balloon’s altitude were blowing at 20 to 30 m/s for most of that week. So it did not go straight, and nothing from the balloon says where it went instead.

We do know how high it was. At float the pressure sensor put it near 280 hPa, between about 9.5 and 10 km, a little lower at night. The weather models publish winds at pressure levels, and a balloon floating at a known pressure is a passive tracer of the wind there. That is the basis of everything below.

The reconstruction sorts the gaps by length. Under 20 minutes is a straight line; at 20 m/s that is at most 24 km. Between 20 minutes and six hours is a particle bridge, described next. Six hours and up is the long-gap method that takes up most of this post. On Stratolink 3 that comes to 246 short gaps, 4 medium ones, and the 4 long ones that hold 92% of the flight.

Winds, cut into a tube

The winds come from NOAA’s Global Forecast System, GFS. It runs four times a day on a 0.25° grid, about 28 km at these latitudes, and publishes wind components on standard pressure levels, hourly out to five days. We read the east and north wind at the two levels bracketing the balloon’s float pressure, 300 and 250 hPa for this flight, and blend them. For times in the past we use the model’s own analyses, every three hours; for the future, the latest cycle’s hourly forecast.

NOAA puts every run in a public S3 bucket with an index of the byte range of each field, so we can fetch just the two wind fields at just the two levels we need, for free and without an API key.1 Each field is still the whole globe, a million grid points, so the per-balloon cube is cut out of it: a stack of time slices, each a small grid of U and V stored as 16-bit integers.

Until this month that cube was one box around the whole mission plus padding. For a flight from California to Spain the box spans an ocean and most of a continent, and to keep it under the point budget the grid had to be coarsened to 0.5° or 1°, throwing away most of the model’s resolution exactly where the balloon spent the most time. The forward forecast could also drift out of the box, and a wind sample outside the box quietly returns the value at the nearest edge. The balloon doesn’t fly on an edge-clamped wind.

The cube is now a tube. Each three-hour slice is its own box, centered on where the track was at that time and wide enough to hold the reconstruction’s particle cloud for a gap of that length: ±9° for short gaps, growing to ±16° for the 201-hour one. Every slice stays at 0.25°. There are two cubes per balloon: a three-hourly one along the whole mission for the reconstruction, 5.8 MB for Stratolink 3, and an hourly one from the last fix forward for the forecast, 1.8 MB.

Short gaps

For a gap between 20 minutes and six hours the balloon can’t have gone far, and the bridge is simple. Solve first for the small speed and heading correction that makes a path driven by the wind land exactly on the next fix. Then launch 200 particles from each fix, each with its own random speed factor, heading offset and altitude; run the forward ones toward the far fix and the backward ones through time-reversed winds toward the near one; weight each by how close it lands to its target, with a 35 km tolerance; and average. The result is a gently curved line that follows the wind and starts and ends on the measured positions.

The eight-day gap

That recipe fails over eight days. Launch particles from New Mexico and hope that some land within a few tens of kilometers of a point in Spain 200 hours later, and none do. The ones that would have needed to draw exactly the right sequence of perturbations for a week, and there aren’t enough particles in the world for that.

The method we had been running tried to get around this with a pull. Each of 600 particles was integrated forward through the winds with a small nudge toward the far fix at every step, and its weight was penalized for every bit of nudging it needed. The trouble is that 600 steps of penalties add up. The log-weights of the particles ended up spanning tens of units, which means one particle holds essentially all of the weight. The line we drew was that one particle’s path, and when the job ran again a different particle won.

I assumed the fix was more particles. It isn’t. The effective sample size, the number of particles that actually contribute to the average, was 1 at 600 particles and still 2 at 60,000. The drawn line moved by 600 to 850 km between reruns, measured as the median separation along the gap, at every particle count I tried. More particles make a more expensive lottery.

Meeting in the middle

The replacement drops the pull entirely. A forward filter runs 2,000 particles from the New Mexico fix toward Spain, and a backward filter runs 2,000 from Spain toward New Mexico through time-reversed winds. Neither knows about the other end. Each particle has its own speed factor, a heading offset that also wanders slowly over the week, and an altitude a few hundred meters above or below the day-and-night float model, because a few hundred meters is enough to change the wind. The only weighting inside a filter is a penalty for reading wind from outside the tube, and whenever a filter’s effective sample size drops below half its population it is resampled, so a cloud never collapses onto a few lineages.

The two clouds meet at the midpoint of the gap, around hour 100, and overlap for eight hours either side of it. Every forward particle is paired with every backward particle, and each pair is weighted by how closely its two halves track each other across the overlap. Two halves that cross at the midpoint heading in different directions pull apart over those eight hours, so the match couples direction as well as position. Inside the window the halves are blended so the joined path is continuous.

Those pair weights are the posterior. 2,000 forward particles times 2,000 backward ones is four million candidate bridges for the cost of integrating two clouds, and on this gap about 23,500 of them carry meaningful weight. The right panel above shows three reruns: they agree to within 65 to 100 km along the gap, against 600 to 850 km before, and the smoother takes about a second and a half to run.

What a particle assumes

Each particle moves with the GFS wind at its altitude, interpolated between the four surrounding grid points and the two surrounding time slices, in 20-minute steps. Its speed factor is drawn with a 12% spread and its heading offset with a 14° spread, once per particle; the heading then takes a random step every four hours, larger for gaps whose endpoints are much closer together than the wind would have carried the balloon. The altitude offset has a 250 m spread around a float model of 9,750 m by day and 9,450 m by night.

None of this models the balloon beyond its altitude. A balloon at 280 hPa is treated as a passive tracer of the wind at 280 hPa, and the perturbations stand in for everything the model doesn’t resolve, from the altitude we don’t measure during the gap to the wind between grid points. The backward filter is the same code with the sign of time flipped: a tracer that moves with the wind can be run backward exactly.

Which line to draw

Having a posterior is nice, but the dashboard draws one line. The obvious choice is the posterior mean, the weighted average position of every bridge at every step. It was the first thing I drew, and the more I looked at it the less I liked it.

The problem is that the average of several real routes is not a route. By the fifth day of the gap the bridges have sorted themselves into groups. In the figure below, 81% of the samples are in a cluster southeast of Newfoundland, 10% are off to the southwest, and 9% are further east. The mean sits in the empty water between them. At the hours where the posterior splits like this, between 0% and 17% of the samples are within 300 km of the mean. It is a smooth line through places the balloon probably wasn’t.

The alternatives I tried each failed differently. Picking the single most central bridge, the medoid, gives a real route inside the biggest cluster, but it is a discrete choice between near-tied candidates and flips between them: by 100 to 170 km just from redrawing the samples, and by up to 1,000 km on a rerun. Grouping the bridges into route families and taking the biggest family sounds right, but the families weren’t stable either; the biggest one’s share moved between 34% and 76% across runs of the same gap.

What we settled on sits between the two. Find the medoid, take the 10% of bridges that track it most closely over the whole gap, 200 of the 2,000, and average those step by step. The line stays inside the dominant mode, it is as smooth as the mean, and it moves by 67 to 136 km between reruns rather than hundreds.

The dashboard still shows one line, and I want to be clear about what it is: the center of the best-supported bundle of routes, not a measurement. In the middle of this gap the posterior is about 1,900 km wide. The line is our best single guess through a corridor that wide, and showing the corridor is the thing I most want to change next.

Where it goes next

The forward forecast is the same integration pointed at the future, with one change. Instead of perturbing a single wind field, each path rides a different model run. NOAA’s Global Ensemble Forecast System, GEFS, publishes 31 members, each started from slightly different initial conditions, and its machine-learning counterpart AIGEFS publishes another 31.2 We cut a tube for every member and integrate the balloon from the last fix through each one, which gives 62 paths that disagree exactly as much as the weather models do.

The line the dashboard draws is the per-hour median of the members, and the forecast ends at the first hour where the 75th-percentile distance of the members from that median passes 2,000 km. Past that point the members are spread across a continent and the median isn’t predicting anything. For Stratolink 3’s last fix near Málaga on May 29, the members ran east across the Mediterranean, Asia and the Pacific, and their spread crossed 2,000 km on June 9, ten and a half days out, over Utah.

Because Stratolink 3 never sent another position, the stretch from that last fix to now is itself a forecast, and the dashboard draws it as one. The route at the top of Teddy’s June post came from an earlier experiment with this machinery, pushed on for weeks toward a guessed endpoint. The forecast now stops when the models stop agreeing, which is the honest place to stop.

The plumbing

All of this runs on a GitHub Actions runner, four times a day. A run starts by deciding which balloons need work. A balloon whose last fix is more than 15 days old and whose stored forecast already ends at its divergence point cannot change, so it is skipped, and when the fleet is quiet the run plans nothing, downloads nothing and exits. When a balloon is flying, the runner pulls the wind fields it needs from NOAA’s bucket by byte range, builds the two cubes plus a tube per ensemble member, runs the reconstruction and the forecast, and writes one JSON file per balloon, about half a megabyte, to Vercel Blob. The dashboard reads that file and draws.

Each bridge is cached by a hash of its two fixes, the pressure level and the method version, so a rerun only recomputes the gaps that changed. For Stratolink 3 the whole compute takes under five seconds. The ingest takes longer, and almost all of it is waiting on S3 for the 62 member cubes.

Where the code is

Everything is in the Stratolink repository, under web/. The ingest is scripts/gfs_ingest.py, with gefs_ingest.py and aigefs_ingest.py for the ensemble members. The cube format and wind sampling are in lib/wind/windCube.ts, the short and medium gaps in lib/wind/pathReconstruction.ts, the two-filter smoother and the trimmed local mean in lib/wind/pathReconstructionLongGap.ts, and the forecast in lib/wind/monteCarloForecast.ts. The schedule is .github/workflows/gfs-ingest.yml.

What’s next

Three things, in the order I think they matter.

Show the corridor. We have 2,000 plausible routes for every long gap and draw one. I’ve prototyped ribbons, sample bundles and daily uncertainty ellipses on the dashboard map, and none of them is on the site yet, mostly because a band 1,900 km wide across the Atlantic has to be drawn carefully to read as “somewhere in here” rather than as noise.

Altitude. The biggest lever on where a path goes is which pressure level it rides, and during a gap we don’t measure it. Replaying stretches of the flight where we do have fixes, the level that best explains the motion is a little deeper than the one the sensor reports, and more so over longer stretches. The smoother should treat the level as something to estimate per gap rather than a constant.

More fixes. Everything here gets easier when the balloon talks more often, and Teddy’s v1 firmware rejects stale fixes so that a packet with a position means a position. The Baja leg of this flight, where we had a position every few minutes, needed none of this.

The positions we did receive, and the line through them, are on Stratolink 3’s flight. Shepherd’s Baja Run covers the first two days, and Teddy’s Looking for Stratolink 3 picks up after the last packet.
